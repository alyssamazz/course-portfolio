Your First AI Conversation

Listen to this lesson

"Prompting 101" by Anthropic. This one goes deeper than the lesson, so skip ahead if it gets technical.

You know what AI is, roughly how it works, which tools exist and how to use them without causing problems. What remains is the part that only comes from doing it.

A prompt is just the instruction you give. The useful frame is delegation. You are handing a task to a capable new colleague who is quick and widely read, but who has never met you, does not know your company, cannot see your screen and will not ask a follow up question unless you invite one. Everything they need has to be in the message.

Four ingredients

Nearly every weak prompt is missing one of these, and nearly every strong one has all four.

  • Task. The specific thing to produce. "Write", "summarise", "compare", "rewrite", "find the problems in".
  • Context. Who it is for, what it is part of, what has already happened, what matters here.
  • Format. Length, structure, tone. A table, five bullets, three sentences, plain language for a non specialist.
  • Constraints. The boundaries. No jargon, do not invent statistics, stay under 200 words, avoid mentioning pricing.

Steer with the second message

Treat the first response as a draft. Experienced users rarely get what they want on the first attempt, and they do not start over. They correct.

Specific corrections work far better than general dissatisfaction. "Make it better" gives the model nothing to act on. "Cut it to half the length, drop the opening pleasantry, and make the second paragraph concrete with an example" gives it four clear instructions. If something in the draft was good, say so, because otherwise a rewrite may quietly discard it.

Show an example when style matters

If you need output in a particular voice or structure, paste an example of what good looks like and ask for the same treatment applied to new material. One good example usually outperforms three paragraphs describing the style you want. This works because the model is pattern matching, and a concrete pattern is far less ambiguous than an abstract description of one.

Ask for the reasoning on harder problems

For anything involving multiple steps, comparison or analysis, asking the model to work through its reasoning before giving an answer measurably improves quality. It also gives you something to audit. If the conclusion looks wrong you can see which step went astray rather than guessing.

Know when to start fresh

Long conversations accumulate context, and not all of it helps. If a thread has wandered, or the model keeps returning to an early misunderstanding, a new conversation with a clean, well constructed prompt is usually faster than trying to correct course. Remember from Lesson 2 that everything in the thread competes for the same context window.

Common belief

Good prompting means memorising magic phrases and role incantations that unlock hidden capability.

What actually works

Clear task, real context, explicit format, honest constraints, then correcting the draft. Plain specificity beats clever phrasing almost every time.

Mistakes worth avoiding early

  • Asking for something enormous in one shot. Break a large task into stages and review each one. Quality falls off sharply when a single request tries to do too much.
  • Saying "make it better". Name the specific change. Shorter, warmer, more concrete, fewer adjectives, different opening.
  • Describing a style instead of showing one. Paste an example. It is faster to write and far more precise.
  • Accepting the first draft because it sounds polished. Fluency is the one thing these models always deliver. It says nothing about whether the content is right for your purpose.
Deep dive: does "you are an expert" actually help?

Opening with a role, such as "you are an experienced financial analyst", is widely repeated advice. It does something, and it is commonly oversold.

What it genuinely does is shift vocabulary, framing and default assumptions. Ask for an explanation of inflation as an economist and you get different terminology than asking as a primary school teacher. That is a real and useful effect on register and depth.

What it does not do is add knowledge or capability. The model does not know more about finance because you called it an analyst. Everything it can do was fixed during training.

The practical consequence: a role is a reasonable shortcut for setting tone and audience, and it is a poor substitute for the four ingredients. If you find yourself stacking increasingly elaborate personas to fix a disappointing answer, the missing piece is almost always context or constraints, not a better job title.

Decision point

The draft that is nearly right

You asked an assistant for a project update for your leadership team. The draft has a strong opening paragraph, but the middle is vague and it is roughly twice as long as you wanted. What is your next message?

Open the other options to see how those would have played out.

Key takeaways

  • Delegate as if to a capable colleague with no context. Task, context, format and constraints all have to be stated.
  • Correct the draft with specific instructions rather than general dissatisfaction.
  • Show an example when style matters. It beats describing the style you want.
  • Start a fresh conversation when a thread has wandered rather than fighting it.
