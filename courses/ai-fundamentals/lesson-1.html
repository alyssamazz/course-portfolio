Lesson 1 of 5

What Is AI?

Listen to this lesson

"How does artificial intelligence learn?" by Briana Brownell, TED-Ed

Ask ten people what AI means and you will get ten answers. One pictures a robot. One pictures ChatGPT. One pictures the feed that keeps them scrolling past midnight. All three are partly right, which is exactly why the word feels slippery.

By the end of this lesson you will have a working definition, the five terms that unlock most AI conversations, and a realistic sense of what today's systems can and cannot do. No maths, no code.

What AI actually is

Artificial intelligence is software that learns patterns from data and uses those patterns to make predictions or decisions. That is the whole idea. The sophistication lives in the scale. Modern systems learn from billions of examples and pick up patterns far too subtle for a person to write down as rules.

The clearest way to see the difference is to build the same thing twice. Take a spam filter.

The hand written approach: a programmer writes rules. If the subject line contains "free money", mark it as spam. If the sender is not in your contacts, raise suspicion. Rules like these are brittle. Spammers adapt within days, every new trick needs a new rule, and the rules start contradicting each other.

The learned approach: you show the system a few hundred thousand emails that people already sorted into spam and not spam. The system works out for itself which combinations of words, sending patterns, formatting quirks and link structures separate the two groups. Nobody writes the rules down. The system derives them, and it catches tricks the programmer never thought of.

Five terms that unlock most AI conversations

These five carry most of the weight. Once they are clear, the jargon in product announcements and news coverage stops being an obstacle.

  • Model. The trained system itself. Think of it as the finished product of the learning process, a large file full of adjusted numbers that turn an input into an output.
  • Machine learning. The umbrella technique behind almost all modern AI, where behaviour is learned from examples instead of programmed by hand.
  • Training data. The examples the model learned from. Quality, coverage and bias in this data shape everything the model does later, which is why it comes up constantly in questions about fairness and accuracy.
  • Generative AI. A category of model that produces new content such as text, images, audio or code, rather than simply scoring or sorting things that already exist.
  • Large language model, or LLM. A generative model trained on a very large body of text. Claude and ChatGPT are the familiar examples. Lesson 2 opens this one up properly.

Narrow AI, and the thing that does not exist yet

Every AI system in commercial use today is narrow. It does one class of task. A model that plays chess at superhuman level cannot read your email. A model that writes fluent essays cannot drive a car. Skill in one domain does not transfer.

General AI, sometimes called AGI, refers to a system that could learn any intellectual task a person can. It does not exist. Whether it will, and when, is a genuine open debate among researchers rather than a settled fact in either direction. When a headline blurs the two, that is usually the moment to read more carefully.

Where you have already used it

Most people have been using AI daily for years without labelling it that way. Your phone keyboard predicting the next word. A bank flagging an unusual charge within seconds. A maps app rerouting around traffic that has not been reported yet. A photo library grouping every picture of the same face. Voice dictation. Automatic captions on a video call.

None of these announce themselves as artificial intelligence, because once a technology works reliably people stop calling it AI and start calling it the feature name. That pattern is old enough to have a nickname among researchers: the AI effect.

Common belief

AI understands what it is saying the way a person understands it, so if the answer sounds thoughtful it must be grounded in real comprehension.

What is actually happening

The system models statistical relationships in its training data. Output can be accurate, fluent and genuinely useful with no comprehension behind it, which is why verification stays your job.

Mistakes worth avoiding early

  • Treating AI as one product. A coding assistant, an image generator and a fraud detector share underlying techniques and almost nothing else. Asking "is AI any good" is like asking "is software any good".
  • Reading confidence as accuracy. These systems produce fluent text whether or not the content is correct. Tone carries no information about reliability.
  • Settling on magic or useless. Both extremes stop you from evaluating a specific tool for a specific job, which is the only question that ever matters in practice.
Deep dive: neural networks are decades old, so why did everything change recently?

The core idea of a neural network dates to the 1950s, and the training method that makes them practical was well understood by the late 1980s. The field then spent years in a slump because the ideas outpaced what the hardware and the available data could support.

Three things shifted. First, the internet produced training data at a scale nobody previously had. Second, graphics chips built for video games turned out to be very well suited to the maths behind training, cutting costs enormously. Third, a 2017 paper from Google introduced the transformer architecture, which processes an entire passage at once instead of word by word and scales far better with added data and compute.

Every current large language model descends from that third change. The "GPT" in ChatGPT stands for Generative Pre-trained Transformer, which is a compact summary of all three shifts.

Key takeaways

  • AI is software that learns patterns from data instead of following rules a person wrote out by hand.
  • Every system in use today is narrow. General AI does not exist, and headlines often blur the two.
  • Generative AI and large language models are one specific branch of a much wider field.
  • Fluent, confident output carries no guarantee of accuracy.
