How AI Models Work

Listen to this lesson

"How Large Language Models Work" by IBM Technology

You do not need the maths to use AI well. You do need a rough mental model, because it tells you what these systems are good at, where they fail, and why they fail in the particular ways they do.

This lesson covers four ideas: how training works, how text becomes something a computer can process, how output gets generated one piece at a time, and why the same question can produce different answers.

Training is one small correction, repeated

A model starts out useless. Its internal settings, called parameters, are effectively random, and its first predictions are nonsense. Training fixes this through a loop that is conceptually simple.

Show the model an example. Let it predict. Measure how wrong the prediction was. Nudge every parameter slightly in the direction that would have made it less wrong. Repeat.

One pass changes almost nothing. The scale is what does the work. A frontier language model has hundreds of billions of parameters and runs that loop across trillions of words of text. The result is a system that has absorbed grammar, factual associations, reasoning patterns, writing styles and a great deal of the structure of human knowledge, without anyone having written a single rule about any of it.

Text has to become numbers first

Models do arithmetic, so language has to be converted into numbers. Text is chopped into tokens, which are chunks roughly three quarters the length of an average English word. Common words are usually one token. Longer or rarer words split into several.

Each token maps to a number, and each number maps to a long list of values that positions the token in relation to every other token the model knows. Words used in similar contexts end up positioned near each other, which is how a model can tell that "physician" and "doctor" are closely related without being told.

Output arrives one token at a time

When you send a prompt, the model does not compose a whole answer and hand it over. It predicts the single most likely next token, appends it, then re-reads everything including what it just wrote and predicts the next one. That loop runs until the response is finished.

At every step the model produces a probability across its entire vocabulary. Given "The capital of France is", the token "Paris" might carry 97% of the probability, with the remainder spread thin across thousands of alternatives.

Why the same question gives different answers

If the model always took the highest probability token, output would be identical every time and noticeably flat and repetitive. Instead it samples from the distribution, and a setting usually called temperature controls how adventurous that sampling is.

Low temperature stays close to the most likely token, which suits factual work and code. Higher temperature reaches further down the list, which produces more varied and creative writing along with more opportunities to go wrong. This sampling step is the direct reason repeating a prompt can produce a different answer, and it is expected behaviour rather than a defect.

The context window is the working memory

A model can only consider a fixed amount of text at once, called the context window, measured in tokens. Everything in a conversation, your prompt, any pasted document and the model's own replies, competes for that space.

Nothing outside the window exists as far as the model is concerned. This explains a common frustration: in a very long conversation, details from early on stop being reflected in the answers. The model has not forgotten in any human sense. Those tokens simply fell outside what it can currently see.

Common belief

The model looks up answers in a stored database of facts, so a wrong answer means the database has an error in it.

What is actually happening

There is no database. Facts are dissolved into statistical patterns across billions of parameters. A wrong answer usually means a plausible pattern won out over an accurate one.

Mistakes worth avoiding early

  • Reporting varied output as a bug. Sampling makes variation the default. Ask instead whether the task needs determinism, and lower the temperature if the tool exposes that control.
  • Pasting an enormous document and expecting full recall. If it does not fit the context window it is not being read. Chunk long material and work through it in pieces.
  • Assuming more parameters always means better. A smaller model tuned for your task often beats a larger general one, at a fraction of the cost and latency.
Deep dive: what attention actually does

The 2017 transformer paper was titled "Attention Is All You Need", and attention is the mechanism that made modern language models practical.

Earlier architectures read text strictly in sequence, which made them slow to train and prone to losing track across long passages. Attention lets the model weigh the relevance of every token against every other token in the window simultaneously.

Take the sentence "The trophy did not fit in the suitcase because it was too big." Resolving what "it" refers to requires connecting that token back to "trophy" rather than "suitcase". Attention assigns a high weight to that specific pairing. Stack this operation across many layers and the model builds up a rich representation of how everything in the passage relates.

Because these comparisons happen in parallel rather than in sequence, training scales across thousands of chips at once. That parallelism, more than any single conceptual leap, is what made models of this size economically possible.

Key takeaways

  • Training is a simple correction loop run at enormous scale, not a set of authored rules.
  • Text becomes tokens, and generation happens one token at a time using probabilities.
  • Sampling is why repeated prompts give varied answers, and temperature controls how varied.
  • The context window is the model's entire working memory. Anything outside it is invisible.
