Types of AI Tools You'll Encounter

Listen to this lesson

"AI, Machine Learning, Deep Learning and Generative AI Explained" by IBM Technology

Almost everything sold as an AI tool falls into one of five categories. Learning the categories matters more than learning product names, because products churn constantly while the categories have stayed stable and tell you what a tool is actually built to do.

Conversational assistants

Claude and ChatGPT are the familiar examples. You describe what you need in ordinary language and get a response. These are the generalists, and they handle a genuinely wide range: drafting and editing, summarising, explaining unfamiliar material, brainstorming, reformatting messy data, translating, and working through a problem out loud with you.

If you only ever learn one category, learn this one. It covers the largest share of everyday knowledge work, and skill here transfers directly to every other category.

Image and video generators

Tools such as Midjourney, DALL-E and Google's image models turn a text description into a picture. Video generation is newer and moving quickly. These are strong for concept art, illustration, mockups, storyboards and marketing visuals.

Two practical limits are worth knowing upfront. Text rendered inside generated images is often garbled, and precise composition takes considerable iteration. Treat the first output as a starting point rather than a deliverable.

Coding assistants

Claude Code, GitHub Copilot and similar tools live inside an editor or terminal and are tuned specifically for software work. They read your existing code for context, which is the key difference from pasting snippets into a chat window. They write new code, explain unfamiliar code, find bugs, write tests and handle repetitive refactoring.

Embedded AI features

Increasingly the AI is inside a product you already pay for. Drafting a reply in your email client, summarising a recorded meeting, suggesting a spreadsheet formula, cleaning up a document. Same underlying technology, packaged for one job and placed exactly where that job happens.

These are often the easiest wins in an organisation, because there is no new tool to procure, no new login, and the data usually stays inside a vendor relationship that has already been reviewed.

Agents

The newest category. An agent is given a goal rather than a single instruction, and it takes multiple steps on its own: searching, calling other software, checking its own work, and retrying. Booking travel against a set of constraints, or working through a backlog of support tickets, are typical framings.

Capability here is advancing fast and reliability varies a great deal by task. The sensible posture is genuine interest paired with close supervision, particularly anywhere an agent can take an action that is hard to reverse.

Off the shelf, configured, or custom built

Cutting across all five categories is a second question: how much of the tool is yours. Most teams should start at the left of this spectrum and only move right when a specific limitation forces it.

Common belief

Pick the single most capable model available and use it for everything. Anything less is settling.

What experienced teams do

Match the tool to the task. A smaller, faster, cheaper model often wins on high volume routine work, and the frontier model gets reserved for jobs that genuinely need it.

Mistakes worth avoiding early

  • Choosing the product before defining the output. Name what you need to end up with, and the category is usually obvious. Skipping that step is how teams end up with three overlapping subscriptions.
  • Jumping straight to custom built. Most requirements that feel unique are met by an off the shelf tool with a good prompt and a few uploaded documents, at a tiny fraction of the cost.
  • Giving an agent irreversible permissions on day one. Sending email, moving money and deleting records deserve a human confirmation step until you have genuinely earned confidence in the setup.
Deep dive: what vendors mean by fine-tuning and RAG

Two terms come up constantly in sales conversations, and the difference between them changes what you should expect.

Fine-tuning takes an existing trained model and continues training it on a narrower dataset, which adjusts the model's own parameters. It is well suited to teaching a consistent style, format or tone. It is a poor way to teach facts, because the knowledge is baked in at training time and updating it means running the process again.

Retrieval augmented generation, usually shortened to RAG, leaves the model untouched. When a question arrives, the system searches your documents, pulls the relevant passages, and places them into the prompt as context. The model answers from material it can actually see.

For the very common request "make it answer questions about our internal documentation", RAG is almost always the right answer. It is cheaper, the source of any claim can be cited, and updating the knowledge means updating a document rather than retraining anything. A vendor proposing fine-tuning for a factual lookup problem is worth questioning closely.

Key takeaways

  • Five categories cover the field: chat assistants, image and video generators, coding assistants, embedded features, and agents.
  • Define the output you need first. The category, then the product, follow from that.
  • Off the shelf beats custom built for most requirements. Move right along that spectrum only when something specific forces it.
  • RAG suits factual questions over your own documents. Fine-tuning suits style and format.
