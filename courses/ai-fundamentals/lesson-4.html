Using AI Responsibly

Listen to this lesson

"Why Large Language Models Hallucinate" by IBM Technology

This is the lesson that keeps you out of trouble. AI tools are genuinely useful and they fail in specific, predictable ways. Once you know where the failures cluster, you can check the right things without slowing yourself down checking everything.

Hallucinations, and where they cluster

A hallucination is confident output that is simply false. Lesson 2 explained the cause: the model produces statistically plausible continuations, and plausible is not the same as true. When the training data thins out, the model does not stop or express doubt. It generates the shape of an answer, filled in with something that fits the pattern.

The useful part is that hallucinations are not evenly distributed. They concentrate around a handful of specific things:

  • Citations and references. Paper titles, case law, page numbers and DOIs are highly patterned, which makes them very easy to fabricate convincingly.
  • Precise figures and dates. Statistics, market sizes and revenue numbers often arrive looking authoritative and sourced when they are neither.
  • URLs. A plausible looking link to a page that has never existed is extremely common.
  • Quotes attributed to people. Especially quotes that sound exactly like something the person would say.
  • Niche or very recent topics. Thin training coverage means more gap filling.

Notice what is missing from that list. Rephrasing text you supplied, summarising a document in the context window, changing tone, brainstorming, and explaining a widely documented concept are all comparatively low risk, because the model is working from material in front of it rather than reaching into its training data.

Privacy: know what you are pasting

Public AI tools may retain conversation data, and terms vary by provider and by plan. Consumer tiers and enterprise agreements often differ substantially on whether your input can be used to improve models.

Rather than memorising policies, sort what you are about to paste.

A quick test that works well in practice: would you be comfortable if this text appeared in a screenshot shared outside your organisation? If the honest answer is no, it needs a policy check before it goes anywhere near a public tool.

Bias, and why it is structural

Models learn from text produced by people, and that text carries the assumptions and imbalances of the world that produced it. A model asked to describe a nurse or an engineer may reproduce occupational stereotypes it absorbed. A résumé screener trained on past hiring decisions can inherit whatever patterns those decisions contained.

Developers work hard to reduce this and none of them have eliminated it. The practical implication is narrow and important: be especially careful using AI output to make or justify decisions about people. Hiring, lending, admissions, performance review and anything similar deserves human ownership of the decision and a record of the reasoning that does not begin and end with "the model said so".

Say when you used it

Norms are still forming, but a reasonable default is to disclose AI involvement when someone's judgement of your work would change if they knew. Using AI to tidy your grammar rarely needs a note. Using it to produce the substance of an analysis someone will rely on usually does. Many organisations, journals and schools now have explicit policies, and it is worth knowing yours before the question comes up rather than after.

Common belief

If the AI provides a source, a link or a citation, the claim has been checked and is safe to repeat.

What is actually happening

Citations are among the easiest things to fabricate convincingly, because they follow a rigid format. A provided source raises your obligation to click it, rather than removing it.

Mistakes worth avoiding early

  • Verifying everything at the same intensity. Exhausting and unsustainable, and it is why people quietly stop checking anything. Use the ladder above.
  • Trusting a citation because it looks properly formatted. Format is exactly what the model is best at reproducing. Click the link or search the title.
  • Pasting first and asking about policy afterwards. Data cannot be unsent. The check takes seconds and belongs before the paste.
  • Letting a model make a decision about a person. Use it to gather and structure information. Keep the judgement, and the accountability, with a human.
Deep dive: the case that made this concrete

In 2023 a New York federal court sanctioned two lawyers after they submitted a legal brief citing several court decisions that did not exist. The citations had been produced by ChatGPT, and they carried convincing case names, reporter volumes and page numbers. When opposing counsel could not locate the cases, the fabrication came to light. The judge imposed a five thousand dollar penalty.

What makes the episode instructive is not that the tool failed. It is that the failure was invisible without checking. The citations looked exactly like real ones, because reproducing that format is precisely what these models excel at. Nothing in the output signalled low confidence.

Similar incidents have followed in other jurisdictions and other professions. The pattern repeats whenever someone treats formatted, confident output as verified output. One click on each citation would have prevented every one of them.

Key takeaways

  • Hallucinations cluster around citations, figures, URLs and quotes. Check those hardest.
  • Match verification effort to consequence rather than checking everything equally.
  • Sort data before pasting. Customer records, credentials and anything under NDA stay out of public tools.
  • Keep human ownership of any decision about a person.
