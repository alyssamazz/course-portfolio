The Confidentiality Trap

A rushed client call, a helpful instinct, and a tax return pasted into a free AI tool. Nobody did anything malicious. The firm is exposed anyway.

12 min film 3 checkpoints Hands on exercise Certificate

What you will be able to do

  • Name the categories of client data that must never enter a public AI tool.
  • Apply a one-sentence test to a live client question, under time pressure.
  • Rewrite an identifying prompt into one that is safe and still useful.

This module does not run on its own. Each act ends with a question, and the next act opens when you answer it. Watching a film and recognising the lesson is not the same as being able to retrieve it on a Tuesday in April with a client on speakerphone — the questions are what make the difference.

Act 1

The Mistake

4 min

Dana owns a small CPA and tax advisory firm. Renata, a retail client, calls on her lunch break about an IRS notice she does not understand and wants a straight answer before she has to get back to the shop.

Dana opens a free public AI tool and pastes in the notice — and, for context, Renata's prior year return. Social Security number, business EIN, reported income, all of it. Then she stops.

“Would I say this out loud, in this room, to this person?”

Dana did not do anything a rushed, caring professional would not do. The instinct to give the tool context — paste the whole return so it understands the situation — is exactly what turns a quick question into a disclosure. The tool never needed to know it was Renata, or that Renata existed, to explain what the notice means.

Act 2

The Consequence

3 min
Answer Checkpoint 1 above and this act opens.

The next morning Grace, the senior partner, comes in holding a printed alert. The firm's monitoring flagged an outbound message overnight containing a Social Security number pattern, sent to an AI domain nobody had reviewed. It came from Dana's login.

Grace is not accusatory. She is precise, which is worse and more useful.

What was actually at stake

  1. The disclosure already happened. Federal law restricts a preparer from disclosing return information without the client's consent. That the tool was not malicious does not enter into it. The exposure occurred the moment the data left approved systems.
  2. Nobody knows where it lives now. If that tool retains or logs what is pasted into it, the firm cannot say where Renata's return is, or for how long.
  3. It is a trust conversation waiting to happen. If Renata ever found out — whether or not anything was ever done with her data — that is a conversation with her, and probably with others once word travels.

None of that requires anything bad to have occurred. That is the part worth carrying: waiting to see whether something happens is not a safeguard.

Checkpoint 2

Grace says the exposure existed “the moment it left our systems.” In practice, what does that mean?

Act 3

The Fix

3 min
Answer Checkpoint 2 above and this act opens.

A few days later Renata calls again — different question, same time pressure. Can she claim home office expenses now that she works from the back room?

Dana types: “What are the current IRS eligibility rules for the home office deduction for a small retail business owner?”

Notice what changed. The question is specific enough to be useful and general enough that it could belong to any client, or to no client at all. Then she closes the tool, opens Renata's actual file herself, and checks the rule against Renata's real square footage.

Same client. Same deadline pressure. Same instinct to be fast and precise. The only thing that moved is where the client's specific numbers lived — in Dana's own review, not in a prompt.

The safe workflow

  1. Ask first: is this general, or does it need this client's numbers?
  2. General → AI can help. No identifiers, no attachments.
  3. Client-specific → you handle it, or use only a firm-approved tool with the figures anonymised.

Write the Prompt

Hands on

Redact this notice

Below is a client notice. Write the prompt you would actually send to an AI tool to understand it. Your text is checked in this browser — nothing is uploaded, which is the only defensible way to run an exercise about not sending data somewhere it should not go.

NOTICE TYPE: CP2000
TAXPAYER: Marcus Webb
SSN: 900-55-0148
EIN: 00-7734521
TAX YEAR: 2024
SUMMARY: Discrepancy between income reported on return ($84,200) and third-party reporting ($91,750).

Fabricated sample. The SSN uses a range never issued and the EIN prefix is unassigned — this is not anyone's record.

One prompt that works “What does a CP2000 notice mean, and what are the general next steps when there is a discrepancy between income reported on a return and third-party reporting such as a 1099?”

Yours does not have to match this. It has to carry no identifying data and still name the notice type and the nature of the discrepancy — safe and useless is not the goal.

Before you close this

  • Never paste an SSN, EIN, full return, or notice text containing identifying data into a public or unreviewed tool.
  • Ask first: general, or client-specific? General is safe to ask generally. Client-specific stays with you.
  • If you are unsure whether something identifies someone, treat it as if it does.
  • Report an accidental disclosure immediately. Early reporting materially changes what a firm can do about it.

For the firm, not just the individual. A one-page AI use policy your staff can actually follow is the companion to this module — the habit holds better when it is written down and someone owns it.
